Every choice Jev makes,
behind one FPS squad.

A commander talks. Five agents move. In between sits a stack of typed questions. There is no parser, no intent classifier, no command grammar. Each question hands Jev a state object and a set of named options, and gets back a probability over them.

This page lays out the whole space: what can be asked, what can be answered, which options even exist at a given moment, and, with a mic open all round, what gets thrown away.

01 Two layers, different clocks

One loop listens to a person. The other plays the game. They meet at one object: the order an agent is carrying.

450ms
Per-agent think interval, staggered so five agents don't fire at once.
26
Questions in one command call: 4 per living agent, plus 6 about the utterance itself.
12
Distinct agent actions in the vocabulary, though never all at once. See 05.
4
Independent channels decided per tick: what to do, where to look, whether to fire, whether it's done.

02 Finding the signal in the noise

A hands-free mic is open the whole round. Almost none of it is an order. The first job is not understanding. It is working out whether there is anything to understand.

There is a failure here that is easy to miss. A squad order phrased as a flat statement does not look like a command, and Jev agrees:

"Alpha and Bravo hold B site"

without the hint 23%
with the hint 94%

The hint is one sentence added to the question: "even when it is phrased as a plain statement." Without it the order reads as a description of what the squad is already doing, scores 23%, and is silently discarded. The 23% is measured and recorded in the source; the 94% is illustrative of the corrected behaviour.

decision line at 0.50

Three outcomes, and two of them can be true at once

The gate is not a router. An utterance is scored for both jobs, and a sentence that does both does both. "they're on B, everyone rotate" is a sighting and an order, and the squad's heads turn as well as their feet.

UtteranceVerdictWhat actually happens
"rotate b"Order Terse game call. Tells them where to go, so it moves people.
"two on b"Callout Nobody is given an order and nobody moves. What changes is the angle agents hold once they've arrived, which is most of a round.

everyone rotate"		Order Callout Both halves are true, so both are applied.

Why the ignore path matters. Every dropped utterance is a squad that didn't run somewhere because you cleared your throat. A gate that lets too much through does not miss orders. It gets you five agents obeying your muttering.

03 Many words, one move

The vocabulary is deliberately wide so a commander never has to remember which word the system knows. The set of things that can actually happen stays small.

Each option is described to Jev by the phrasings people really shout, not by a tidy verb. The lists grew from wordings that came back wrong: "rotate to a" was read as a flank, "camp b" as a push, "on me" as a push.

The hard cases are positional, not lexical

"a push b"

Alpha goes to the B site. A lone letter is an agent when it's the subject of a clause and a site when it's the destination. Position decides, and only an order verb following the letter makes it a name, which keeps the article out of "throw a nade" and "take a peek".

"A site, no, B site"

A mid-sentence correction. The last place they land on wins. Same rule covers "A or B" and a trailing-off "A site… B site".

"Charlie rush A, Alpha plant"

Two jobs in one breath. Each agent is asked separately whether any part of the order applies to it, so one utterance can fan out into different orders.

"everyone else hold"

Means the squad minus whoever was just named, which can only be resolved against the clause that named them. A pattern can spot the phrase; it can't work out who is left.

Where, and which way there

Destination and route are separate questions, because a commander routinely says both: "mid to B through doors". Every zone on the map is offered as a possible node to travel through, described by what people call it, so "through cat" and "via doors" resolve the same way a destination does. Beyond the named zones, the target space also carries:

TargetMeaningExists when
pointedExactly where the commander is pointing.A pointer was given

forwardA short step further the way they're already facing. Resolved per agent against where it stands, not against the map.Always

A relative target is a relation, not a coordinate: the simulation works out where that actually is, and falls back to standing still if there is nowhere to go. It is why one utterance, "everyone move up a bit", can mean five different points.

04 The action space

Two vocabularies. What a commander can order, and what an agent can decide to do on its own.

The last two are behind a settings toggle, so an ordinary match offers Jev exactly the vocabulary it always had, plus the two moves the squad could always physically make and had no word for.

How many throw?

One smoke is a smoke. Five smokes on one doorway is the squad's whole round spent at once. So "how many" is its own graded question rather than a side effect of who was addressed. "three people smoke main" is a number the commander said out loud, and it should be obeyed as one.

Layer 2: what an agent can choose

These are the agent's own options during contact. The commander's order outranks them: the question says so in as many words: carry it out unless doing so right now would get you killed, or you cannot carry it out from here.

ActionMeans
advanceKeep moving to the ordered position. Labelled as "carry out your order" when it is the one the order implies.
holdStay put and watch this angle.
fightStop and shoot. Standing still makes you far more accurate, but it puts the order on hold.
coverBreak line of sight. Only when hurt and outnumbered.
nadeThrow at a bunched group: hurts all of them, so it beats shooting one.
flashNo damage, but for a few seconds they can't see, which is how you take an angle someone is holding.
smokeBlocks the sightline both ways, so you can cross open ground or break a fight you're losing.
scatterRun clear of a grenade about to go off.
supportGo help a teammate who is in a fight.
repositionAlready where you were sent: move off the angle you're watched from, without leaving.
peekLean out and step straight back, making them show you where they are without standing there.
knifeFaster with it out, kills outright from behind, can't shoot while holding it.

05 Scope: the menu is rebuilt every tick

The action space is assembled per agent, per tick, out of that agent's situation. Nothing impossible or suicidal is on the menu to be chosen in the first place.

advancealways, with the live distance to the objective written into the option text
holdalways
coveralways, but the text restricts it: only when you are hurt and outnumbered
fightan enemy is in sight
nade2+ enemies bunched within 5 m and no grenade already incoming
flashutility on · one held · nearest enemy ≥ 10 m · no incoming nade · nobody already blinded
smokeutility on · one held · enemy in sight · no incoming nade · a viable spot exists
scattera grenade is about to go off beside you
supporta teammate is fighting and you can see no enemy yourself
repositionwithin 3 m of where you were sent
peeknearest enemy > 12 m and no incoming nade
knifenearest enemy ≤ 6 m and no incoming nade

Read the two distance gates together: peek needs > 12 m, knife needs ≤ 6 m. They can never both be offered. A flash needs ≥ 10 m because its blast reaches wider than its throw. One popped in someone's face blinds you rather than them, so it is only a choice when there is room for it to be one.

06 Four channels, decided in parallel

Where the feet go, where the weapon points, whether to fire, and whether the job is done are four different decisions, so they are four different questions.

action
choice · the menu above
What the feet do. The only one that can put the commander's order on hold.
gaze
choice · 5 options
Where the weapon points: travel · on_threat · hold_angle · scan · watch_back. Separate because you can fall back while still watching the doorway you're falling back from.
hold_fire
boolean · at 0.6
Whether to give your position away. Automatic fire is the rule; choosing not to is what makes an ambush, a lurk, and letting one walk past possible at all.
order_complete
boolean · at 0.6
The terminator. Without it an agent that arrived somewhere simply stopped existing until an enemy appeared. Flips the job from taking ground to holding it.
target
choice · only if 2+ visible
Which enemy first, each described by distance, HP, and whether they're already shooting at you.

Before that, one more thing worth naming: an agent that has nothing to decide skips all of it. No enemy in sight, no teammate fighting, no grenade incoming, and agentTick() returns null. The agent follows its order, and no question is asked. Standing frozen used to come from exactly here.

Proposals, not commands. The channels can contradict each other and the world can contradict both. The simulation vetoes a frame that doesn't make sense, the same way a reflex outranks a judgement. Gaze is a suggestion about where to look, not a guarantee the head gets there.

07 Unstructured, continuous, messy input

Nobody says "walk there, spread out." They say it by how they say it. The unspoken half of an order is read from voice signal and turned into movement.

What the question refuses to conclude. Loudness alone is not urgency. Swearing alone doesn't make speech an order or prove emotion. Profanity can reinforce an explicit urgent order, but it cannot create one. These are written into the instructions as negatives, because the measurements are exactly the kind a model will happily over-read.

A sharp edge learned the hard way: the questions point at what was just said, not at current_orders. Aim them at the standing order and Jev answers with the order the squad already had, whatever you shouted. While pushing B, "nade mid" came back as a grenade on B. The history is in the state so a follow-up can be resolved, and nowhere else.

08 Where the cut-offs are

Every answer is a distribution. Somewhere it has to turn into something that happens.

QuestionCutBelow it
is_order0.50Nobody moves. The utterance is still checked for a callout first.
addresses_everyone0.50Fall through to asking each agent separately. Above it, every per-agent answer is overridden to "yes".
<agent>_addressed0.50That agent is skipped with the reason "not addressed". The rest of the squad still acts.
is_callout0.60No sighting is recorded. Higher than the order gate: a false callout turns five heads for nothing.
hold_fire0.60Shoot. The default is to fire; silence has to be argued for.
order_complete0.60Keep advancing. Only flips advance to hold, never clears the order.

Four reasons an agent ignores an order it was given

Being addressed is only the first hurdle. Each skip records its own reason, and they show up in the squad view rather than being silently swallowed:

ReasonWhen
not addressedThe addressed probability came in under 0.5.
agent eliminatedDead between the utterance and the answer coming back.
round endedThe round resolved mid-flight.
newer order already appliedA later command landed first. Sequence numbers stop a partial voice guess from overwriting the finished sentence it came from.

That last one is a real hazard of streaming speech: the interim transcript "alpha go" and the final "alpha go b" are two commands racing each other. The sequence number settles it regardless of which returns first.

09 Judged by Jev, or settled in code

The line is not "hard problems go to the model". It is spelling versus judgement.

Settled in code

Expanding initials. "c hold mid" → "Charlie hold mid". One spelling for the state Jev reads and the history a later "do the same" resolves against. If two agents share an initial it stays a letter and Jev decides.

Thresholds, gating, sequencing, stand-downs. Anything with a right answer that doesn't depend on what the words meant.

Judged by Jev

Who an order is for, including the whole-squad phrasings. Asked as addresses_everyone, one squad-level question that wins over the per-agent ones: if the order was for all of them it was for this one, whatever its own question happened to say.

A pattern can match the word "everyone". It cannot work out that "Alpha and everyone push" is a mix, or that "everyone else hold" means the squad minus a name in the previous clause. Matching the word and knowing who it covers are different problems.

This boundary moved recently, and it moved toward Jev. Whole-squad addressing used to be a regex over everyone|everybody|guys|all of you|y'all|…, with a second pattern for the "everyone else" exception. It was a real saving: it settled in one test what would otherwise cost a question per agent, and five separate questions are five chances to disagree about a phrase with one meaning.

It came out anyway, for two reasons. A word list can only ever contain the phrasings somebody thought of in advance. And it had quietly become two authorities on the same word: a pattern saying "everyone means all five", and a graded throw_headcount question saying how many should actually throw. On "everyone throw your flashes" both read the same word and could return different answers, with the winner decided by the order the code happened to check them in. Now one question owns it.

What stayed. expandAgentInitials kept its regexes, and that's the line: rewriting "c" as "Charlie" is spelling and has a right answer. Deciding whether "everyone" includes the agent you just named by name is judgement, and never had one.
